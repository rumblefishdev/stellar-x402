# Sign on Hetzner, cap the damage on Stellar

The safest practical choice for a Hetzner-hosted facilitator is a **self-hosted OpenBao Transit signer on its own Hetzner servers**, paired with Stellar-level controls that matter more than the choice of custody product: thresholds that keep the hot key below **high**, a cold recovery signer on every channel and on the fee account, and a small hot fee balance. OpenBao keeps the raw seed out of the facilitator process, logs every signature, can be revoked at once, and has been shown to work for Stellar first-hand: a testnet transaction signed only by an OpenBao Transit key landed (hash `26a93987…9c86`, ledger 5210493), at about 6 ms per signature on localhost ([spike results](../spike/results.md)). AWS KMS is now a credible alternative. It has supported pure Ed25519 since November 2025, documents seed import, costs a few dollars a month, and gives a stronger custody guarantee, since nobody, including root on our own servers, can extract the key. But from Hetzner it puts a cross-cloud call, an AWS identity bootstrap and a second vendor's outage domain on every settlement. It has also never been run against Stellar, by us or by anyone we could find. No managed or hardware option changes the key fact: **whoever controls the facilitator host can use the key while they are there.** Only Stellar thresholds bound what that use can do. Our channels today leave every threshold at 0, so the hot key alone can rewrite signers on every channel. That gap is the most urgent finding in this report, whichever custody option is chosen.

## The Ed25519 requirement narrows the field to three real options

Stellar signs the 32-byte transaction hash with pure Ed25519 (RFC 8032). A signer has to accept those 32 bytes as the message and run SHA-512 inside the signature. One that signs a pre-computed digest, or only does Ed25519ph (HashEdDSA), produces signatures Stellar rejects. That single requirement removes most of the market. **Hetzner offers no KMS, HSM or secrets manager** in either Cloud or Robot ([Hetzner Docs](https://docs.hetzner.com/); [Hetzner Cloud](https://www.hetzner.com/cloud)). The EU clouds usually paired with it do not help. Scaleway Key Manager signs only with EC P-256/P-384 and RSA ([Scaleway](https://www.scaleway.com/en/docs/key-manager/reference-content/cryptographic-details-key-manager/)), OVHcloud KMS only with RSA and NIST ECDSA ([OVHcloud](https://docs.ovhcloud.com/en/guides/manage-and-operate/kms/kms-usage)), and no official STACKIT signing list turned up. **Azure Key Vault and Managed HSM are excluded in principle**: they offer no EdDSA, and their Sign is "strictly … sign hash", which pure Ed25519 cannot use ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/key-vault/keys/about-keys-details)). Thalassa Cloud, a small Dutch provider, claims Ed25519 in its KMS ([Thalassa](https://docs.thalassa.cloud/docs/kms/sign-verify-hmac/)), but its protection level and its pure-versus-prehash mode were not checked.

Hardware on Hetzner fails for practical reasons rather than cryptographic ones. A **YubiHSM 2 takes about 105 ms to sign 32 bytes** with Ed25519 ([Yubico device specs](https://docs.yubico.com/hardware/yubihsm-2/hsm-2-user-guide/hsm2-device-specs.html)). That is roughly 9–10 signatures per second per device, against our peak of about 20 per second (100 signatures per 5 s ledger), and Hetzner documents no USB passthrough or HSM add-on for its servers ([Hetzner Robot](https://docs.hetzner.com/robot/general/overview)). The Nitrokey NetHSM is fast enough, at about 3,720 Ed25519 signatures per second ([Nitrokey](https://www.nitrokey.com/node/414)), but it is a rack appliance. Hetzner's colocation pages conflict: one offers racks in Nuremberg, Falkenstein and Helsinki, and another says no space can be provided "until further notice" ([Hetzner colocation](https://www.hetzner.com/colocation?country=at); [DE page](https://www.hetzner.com/de/colocation/?country=no)). The USB Nitrokey HSM 2 does no Ed25519 at all ([Nitrokey forum](https://support.nitrokey.com/t/is-it-possible-to-use-the-nitrokey-hsm-to-create-ed25519-signatures/181)). TPM 2.0 has EdDSA in the TCG algorithm registry ([TCG registry 1.34](https://trustedcomputinggroup.org/wp-content/uploads/TCG-Algorithm-Registry-Revision-1.34_pub-1.pdf)), but we found no evidence that any shipping TPM implements it, and a community report says Hetzner Cloud VMs expose no TPM ([HN comment, unverified](https://news.ycombinator.com/threads?id=Qasaur)). A TPM is therefore useful on dedicated servers only to seal secrets, not to sign.

The managed HSM vendors and custody APIs do support Stellar-compatible signing, but at a cost or friction that rules them out for a hot key. **Fortanix DSM** lists Ed25519 in a "standard" (pure) mode over REST ([Fortanix](https://support.fortanix.com/docs/dsm-algorithm-support)). **Thales Luna Cloud HSM on DPoD** implements `CKM_EDDSA` as PureEdDSA by default, but only through PKCS#11 ([Thales](https://thalesdocs.com/dpod/services/luna_cloud_hsm/extern/client_guides/Content/sdk/luna_cloud_mechanisms/CKM_EDDSA.htm)). **Securosys** supports Ed25519 according to an EJBCA integration guide ([Keyfactor](https://docs.keyfactor.com/ejbca/9.5/securosys-primus-hsm-and-cloudshsm-service)), but this is unconfirmed for its REST API. None of the three publishes a price, so each needs a sales conversation and a trial. Among the custody APIs, **Turnkey** is the cleanest fit: raw Ed25519 payload signing with `HASH_FUNCTION_NOT_APPLICABLE`, and Stellar named as a supported Ed25519 chain ([Turnkey](https://docs.turnkey.com/ecosystems/others); [SDK](https://pub-web-s2.flutter-io.cn/documentation/turnkey_http/1.1.0/__generated___models/TSignRawPayloadsBody-class.html)). But it bills per signature, and the third-party figures of $0.01–$0.10 per signature ([apicostcalc, unverified](https://apicostcalc.com/privy-vs-dynamic-vs-turnkey-wallet-cost-calculator.html)) put even 1M signatures a month at $10k–$100k. **Fireblocks** raw EdDSA signing is a premium add-on that runs through its policy engine ([Fireblocks](https://support.fireblocks.io/hc/en-us/articles/4413379762450)), and a community thread documents failures signing raw Stellar payloads ([Fireblocks community](https://community.fireblocks.com/t/trouble-signing-raw-stellar-transaction/539)). **Dfns** signs whole Stellar transactions ([Dfns](https://docs.dfns.co/api-reference/sign/stellar)), and **Privy** lists Stellar only as a Tier 2 chain ([Privy](https://docs.privy.io/wallets/overview/chains)). These products are built for treasury and user wallets, not for a fee payer that signs on every settlement.

**Google Cloud KMS** is the only other hyperscaler fit. It documents `EC_SIGN_ED25519` as "PureEdDSA mode, which takes raw data", but **only at the SOFTWARE protection level**: Ed25519 does not appear in its HSM tables ([GCP KMS algorithms](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kms/docs/algorithms?hl=en)). Software keys run on BoringCrypto, FIPS 140-2 Level 1 ([GCP protection levels](https://cloud.google.com/kms/docs/protection-levels?hl=ko)). It is cheap, at about $0.03 per 10,000 signatures ([GCP pricing](https://cloud.google.com/kms/pricing)), and Hetzner hosts can reach it through Workload Identity Federation with X.509 certificates ([GCP WIF](https://docs.cloud.google.com/iam/docs/workload-identity-federation-with-x509-certificates)). Whether an existing Stellar seed can be imported was not confirmed. Since GCP's Ed25519 key sits in software, as an OpenBao key does, GCP adds a second cloud without adding hardware custody. That leaves three real options: the key as a host secret, AWS KMS called from Hetzner, and OpenBao Transit on Hetzner.

## AWS KMS now works for Stellar, but every settlement crosses clouds

ADR 0011 left two questions open for KMS: whether its signature output fits Stellar, and whether an existing seed can be imported. Both now have documented answers. Neither has been tested against Stellar.

### Compatibility: pure Ed25519 over the raw 32-byte hash

AWS KMS announced Ed25519 on 2025-11-07, with 64-byte signatures, 32-byte public keys and blockchain named as a use case ([AWS What's New](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2025/11/aws-kms-edwards-curve-digital-signature-algorithm/)). The key spec is `ECC_NIST_EDWARDS25519`, and it offers two algorithms that "cannot be used interchangeably". **`ED25519_SHA_512` with `MessageType: RAW` is FIPS 186-5 §7.6 pure EdDSA**, which is what Stellar needs. `ED25519_PH_SHA_512` is HashEdDSA, and in that mode AWS hashes the input a second time ([KMS key spec reference](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/symm-asymm-choose-key-spec.html)), so Stellar would reject its output. Sign accepts messages of 1–4096 bytes, so the 32-byte hash goes in as is. The API reference documents the signature encoding for RSA and ECDSA but not for EdDSA ([Sign API](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/APIReference/API_Sign.html)). The evidence for raw 64-byte R‖S output comes from AWS's announcement and from production code: the Solana Foundation's `@solana/keychain-aws-kms` v1.4.0 takes `response.Signature` with no DER parsing, rejects anything that isn't 64 bytes, and verifies each signature locally ([source](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/@solana/keychain-aws-kms@1.4.0/dist/aws-kms-signer.js)). Ed25519 is deterministic and FIPS 186-5 adopted RFC 8032, so a KMS signature over `tx.hash()` should match what `Keypair.sign(hash)` produces. `GetPublicKey` returns a DER SPKI ([GetPublicKey API](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/APIReference/API_GetPublicKey.html)), so the G-address is built from its last 32 bytes. A Stellar adapter would therefore call `Sign(RAW, ED25519_SHA_512, tx.hash())`, check that the result is 64 bytes, verify it locally (about 50 µs), and attach it with the key's 4-byte hint. **No one has published a Stellar transaction signed by KMS, and we have not run one**, so a testnet smoke test is a precondition, not a formality.

### Import, export and recovery

**An existing Stellar seed can be imported**, so the facilitator keeps its G-address. Asymmetric keys other than ML-DSA accept imported material, multi-Region keys included ([Importing key material](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/importing-keys.html)). Ed25519 is listed explicitly, as an RFC 5208 PKCS#8 private key wrapped with RSA-OAEP ([import requirements](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/importing-keys-conceptual.html)). No AWS example shows an Ed25519 import end to end, so a check that the G-address survives the import belongs in the smoke test. The private key is never exportable. That creates a real choice. A key generated inside KMS never exists outside the HSMs, but if it is lost (an account closed, or a deletion that nobody cancels), so is the G-address. An imported key keeps an offline copy, which is both the backup and a second place the seed can leak from. Deletion waits 7–30 days and can be cancelled. Deleting only the imported material can be undone by importing it again ([Deleting keys](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/deleting-keys.html)). Multi-Region keys keep the same key, and so the same G-address, in a replica Region, but imported material has to be imported into each replica separately ([Multi-Region keys](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/multi-region-keys-overview.html)).

### Authenticating from Hetzner

Hetzner hosts cannot use instance roles, so the facilitator needs some AWS identity of its own. The cleanest is **IAM Roles Anywhere**: an X.509 certificate from our own CA is exchanged for STS credentials lasting 15 minutes to 12 hours ([Roles Anywhere](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/rolesanywhere/latest/userguide/introduction.html)). The credential helper (v1.8.5) can keep the certificate's private key in a TPM or a PKCS#11 token. In `serve` mode it exposes an IMDS-style endpoint on 127.0.0.1:9911, and AWS warns that any local process can read credentials from it ([credential helper](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/rolesanywhere/latest/userguide/credential-helper.html)). Revocation works only through imported CRLs ([trust model](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/rolesanywhere/latest/userguide/trust-model.html)). The key policy can be tight. It can allow `kms:Sign` only for `ED25519_SHA_512`/`RAW`, only from the Hetzner IPs through `aws:SourceIp` (IPv6 ranges must be listed too, or IPv6 callers are not denied), and only for the facilitator's certificate CN. Admin actions such as `ScheduleKeyDeletion` and `PutKeyPolicy` go to a separate break-glass principal ([KMS condition keys](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/conditions-aws.html)). VPC-endpoint conditions cannot apply from outside AWS. A static IAM access key is the fallback, and the worst option: a long-lived secret that works from anywhere unless an IP condition pins it. **The limit in every variant is that KMS cannot see what it signs.** Sign takes no encryption context, and a 32-byte hash is opaque to policy, so a compromised host with a live session can sign any transaction for this key. Checking the content would need a proxy inside AWS that rebuilds the XDR before calling Sign, which adds a second hop.

### Quotas, cost, latency and audit

Capacity and cost are not concerns. Sign on ECC keys shares an adjustable quota of **1,000 requests per second per account and Region**, about 50 times our peak ([quotas](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/requests-per-second.html)). The quota is shared with every other ECC key in the account, so the signer belongs in a dedicated AWS account. Pricing is **$1 per key per month** and, using AWS's ECC example, **$0.15 per 10,000 requests**. Asymmetric Sign is excluded from the free tier ([KMS pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/kms/pricing/)). That comes to about $16 a month for 1M signatures and $151 for 10M. Latency is the open risk. **AWS publishes no Sign latency**, one anecdote reports ~160 ms for P-256 signing from Lambda ([re:Post](https://www.repost.aws/questions/QUPEubdAqrSnSLm-38Vjyqpw/kms-signing-performance-with-asymmetric-ecc-nist-p256-key-is-slow)), and no measured Hetzner-to-Frankfurt RTT was found. Our 10–30 ms p50 from Falkenstein or Nuremberg to eu-central-1 is an unmeasured estimate, against 5.4 ms p50 for OpenBao on localhost. The SLA target is 99.999% per Region ([KMS SLA](https://aws.amazon.com/kms/sla/)), but signing then depends on KMS, STS and the internet path between two providers, and a lost path stops `/settle`. Audit is a real strength. Every Sign is a CloudTrail management event recording key, algorithm, message type, caller and source IP, but not the message ([CloudTrail Sign event](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/kms/latest/developerguide/ct-sign.html)). We would log the transaction hash next to the KMS request ID ourselves.

### FIPS is not covered for Ed25519 today

The KMS HSM holds a FIPS 140-3 Level 3 certificate, **#4884, an interim validation that sunsets on 2026-11-17** ([NIST CMVP](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/cryptographic-module-validation-program/certificate/4884)). Its security policy, for firmware 1.8.104 dated October 2024, lists ECDSA, including secp256k1 "in block-chain related applications", but no EdDSA in the part that was read ([security policy](https://sec-certs.org/fips/9326acebbb07fdc5/target.txt)). Ed25519 shipped a year after that firmware. **The hardware is FIPS-validated; Ed25519 signing is not shown to be inside the boundary.** If FIPS ever becomes a customer requirement, ask AWS about the successor certificate. Today it gives no reason to prefer KMS.

**Verdict on AWS KMS:** viable and cheap, with the strongest anti-exfiltration guarantee of the three. Its costs are a cross-cloud hot path of unmeasured latency, a PKI to run for Roles Anywhere, and the AWS dependency the move to Hetzner was meant to drop. It belongs behind the same `TransactionSigner` interface as a documented upgrade path, not as the default.

## OpenBao Transit gives KMS-style separation inside the private network

Transit's `ed25519` key type does what KMS does for us, and this is the one option backed by first-hand evidence. On Vault 2.1.2 and OpenBao 2.7.1, the spike showed the following: a key generated in Transit gives a raw 32-byte public key, and so a G-address; its signature over the transaction hash is 64 bytes and is accepted by `Keypair.verify` and `tx.addSignature`; an existing `S…` secret imported through BYOK (RSA-OAEP + AES-KWP, PKCS#8) keeps its G-address; a non-exportable key refuses export; and **200 sequential signatures took 5.4 ms p50 and 9.3 ms p99** on OpenBao. A `createAccount` signed only by OpenBao landed on testnet ([spike results](../spike/results.md)). A Transit `TransactionSigner` hashes the XDR, posts the hash to `transit/sign/<key>`, strips the `vault:v1:` prefix and attaches the signature. The pool already accepts any `TransactionSigner`.

**OpenBao beats Vault on licence and on features, not on cryptography.** Vault Community is still under BSL 1.1, and its licensor is now IBM ([jorijn.com](https://jorijn.com/en/blog/hashicorp-vault-vs-openbao/)). Its PKCS#11 HSM seal is Enterprise-only ([Vault PKCS#11 seal](https://www.vaultproject.io/docs/configuration/seal/pkcs11)). OpenBao 2.7 provides a static seal and a Transit seal built in, with the PKCS#11 seal as an open-source plugin ([OpenBao 2.7 release notes](https://openbao.org/community/release-notes/2-7-0/)). The same release added **Transit "external key material"**, so Transit can sign with keys held in KMS- or HSM-backed providers. Whether that path supports Ed25519 is unconfirmed, but if it does, it is a later route to hardware custody that keeps the same API. Running it costs an ongoing patch commitment: 2.7.0 and 2.7.1 fixed 15 advisories between them, mostly in auth (AppRole, Kubernetes, OIDC) and audit-log redaction, not in Transit signing. The last Transit advisory found is CVE-2023-4680, which concerned encryption ([GitLab advisory](https://advisories.gitlab.com/pkg/golang/github.com/hashicorp/vault/CVE-2023-4680/)). Enabling only TLS certificate auth, with no UI or OIDC, removes most of that surface.

Its custody weakness is plain: **OpenBao is software.** On an unsealed node the Transit key is decrypted in memory, so root on the OpenBao server can take it. That server must therefore never be the facilitator host. On a separate server in the private network, with a policy allowing only `transit/sign/<key>` and the audit log shipped off the box, an exploit in the app turns into a signing oracle that ends when access is revoked, not into a stolen seed. The unseal method becomes the new root of trust. Shamir, the default, leaves no secret on any server, but every restarted node waits for a human to unseal it ([OpenBao seal docs](https://openbao.org/docs/configuration/seal/)). The static seal keeps a 32-byte key in a file or env var, and the docs warn to "carefully evaluate" it ([static seal](https://openbao.org/docs/configuration/seal/static/)). Transit auto-unseal from a small separate OpenBao moves the problem one hop away. A PKCS#11 seal on a YubiHSM would be a good use of that device, since it signs once at start-up rather than per settlement, but it depends on Hetzner accepting a USB device. With three Raft nodes, Shamir works with an on-call rota: rolling one node at a time keeps quorum, and only a full-cluster outage needs a key holder. Hetzner spread placement groups keep the nodes on different hardware at no cost ([placement groups](https://docs.hetzner.com/cloud/placement-groups/overview)). After the 2026 price rises, **three CX23 nodes cost about €16.50 a month** (CX23 at €5.49; [privatedevops](https://privatedevops.com/news/hetzner-june-2026-cloud-price-increase-what-to-do)), or about €58 if CX/CAX stock forces CPX22. Recovery is better than with KMS: an encrypted Raft snapshot plus the unseal keys restores the same key, so losing a server does not lose the key.

The host-secret option, `FACILITATOR_SECRET` from an env file, Docker secret, systemd credential or SOPS file, is the baseline and the present code. SOPS, systemd-creds and Docker secrets protect the key at rest, but the seed sits in the process's memory at every signature, so any code execution in the facilitator steals it permanently. Leaks through logs are a proven failure mode: Slope's wallet sent seed phrases to Sentry, and at least 1,444 drained Solana wallets were traced to it ([BankInfoSecurity](https://www.bankinfosecurity.com/seed-phrase-compromise-may-have-caused-solana-wallets-drain-a-19705)). BlackWallet lost ~670k XLM after an attacker got into its hosting-provider account ([BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/hackers-hijack-dns-server-of-blackwallet-to-steal-400-000/)), which is a reminder that the Hetzner console and its rescue mode are themselves exfiltration paths for any key on disk. SDF's own Disbursement Platform does essentially this: AES-GCM-encrypted keys in Postgres, a key-encryption key from an env var, and a limited hot wallet ([SDP signing code](https://github.com/stellar/stellar-disbursement-platform-backend/blob/develop/internal/transactionsubmission/engine/signing/channel_account_db_signature_client.go); [SDP security](https://github.com/stellar/stellar-docs/blob/main/docs/platforms/stellar-disbursement-platform/admin-guide/security.mdx)). So both OpenBao and KMS go beyond Stellar's reference practice, not merely meet it. One facilitator key signs for every channel, which makes our key more valuable than any single SDP key, so the host-secret option is acceptable only on testnet, if at all.

## Stellar thresholds, not the custody product, decide the blast radius

Every option leaves on the facilitator host a credential that can *use* the key: a Transit cert, a Roles Anywhere session, an API token or the seed itself. Custody decides whether a compromise **ends when access ends** (KMS, OpenBao, HSM) or **lasts until an on-chain rotation** (host secret). What the attacker can sign while inside is decided on chain, and today we decide it badly. Our channels are created with the facilitator as signer at weight 1, `masterWeight: 0` and **every threshold left at 0** ([`setup.ts`](../../../../../packages/signer-pool/src/setup.ts)). The facilitator key alone therefore meets **high** on every channel, and a thief can add their own signer, raise the thresholds and lock us out of all channels at once.

Stellar's threshold model fixes this exactly. Changing signers or thresholds and `AccountMerge` are high-threshold operations. Invoke Host Function and Payment are medium. A fee bump needs only the fee account's **low** threshold ([list of operations](https://github.com/stellar/stellar-docs/blob/main/docs/learn/fundamentals/transactions/list-of-operations.mdx); [fee-bump transactions](https://developers.stellar.org/docs/build/guides/transactions/fee-bump-transactions)). On **channels**, set the hot key at weight 1, a cold recovery key at weight 2, and thresholds low 1, medium 1, high 2. The hot key still signs every settlement but can no longer touch signers. On the **fee account**, set the hot key at weight 1, thresholds low 1, medium 2, high 2, and the cold key at least at high. The hot key can then fee-bump but cannot send a Payment out of the account, so a thief can spend the balance only through fees. Stellar's own docs describe this pattern: a key "with a weight below your anchor account's medium threshold" can sign low-threshold operations but cannot make payments ([signatures and multisig](https://github.com/stellar/stellar-docs/blob/main/docs/learn/fundamentals/transactions/signatures-multisig.mdx)). The order of changes matters. Add the recovery signer, then raise high, in one atomic `SetOptions` sequence per account, because weights summing below high lock the account for good. `checkChannel` must change with it: today it flags any other signer as `extra-signer` and checks only medium. It should require facilitator weight ≥ max(low, medium), facilitator weight < high, and recovery weight ≥ high.

**The cold recovery signer is also what makes a non-exportable key safe to use.** Without it, a deleted KMS key or a lost OpenBao cluster freezes every channel and the fee account permanently, since even `AccountMerge` needs high. With it, loss and theft both become routine rotations that need nothing from the hot key. A classic transaction holds up to 100 operations, each with its own source account ([operations and transactions](https://github.com/stellar/stellar-docs/blob/main/docs/learn/fundamentals/transactions/operations-and-transactions.mdx)). One cold signature can therefore add a new hot key to 100 channels, or swap old for new on 50 (two operations per channel), with the recovery account as transaction source so that channel sequence numbers stay untouched. The extra signer costs reserve. Our 1.5 XLM starting balance is exactly (2 + 1 signer) × 0.5, so each channel needs another 0.5 XLM: 150 XLM for 300 channels. The alternative is a cold sponsor account that sponsors reserves with `BeginSponsoringFutureReserves`, which is SDP's pattern for channel creation ([SDP horizon.go](https://github.com/stellar/stellar-disbursement-platform-backend/blob/develop/internal/transactionsubmission/services/horizon.go)). Sponsored channels hold no XLM a thief could drain, at the cost of a cold or warm signature for every new channel.

The last control is money. SDF tells SDP operators to run the wallet "as a hot wallet with a limited amount of funds" ([SDP security](https://github.com/stellar/stellar-docs/blob/main/docs/platforms/stellar-disbursement-platform/admin-guide/security.mdx)). Solana's Kora fee relayer gives the same advice: a minimal operational balance, automated top-ups and alerts on unusual activity ([Kora signers](https://solana.com/docs/tools/kora/operators/signers.md)). Under surge pricing the effective fee becomes the bid ([fees and metering](https://github.com/stellar/stellar-docs/blob/main/docs/learn/fundamentals/fees-resource-limits-metering.mdx)), so a thief with the hot key can burn the fee account's float through fee bumps of their own transactions. The float, topped up by a Payment from a treasury key the facilitator never holds, is what caps that loss. No public figure for the "right" float size exists. Size it to hours of peak settlement fees, and alert on burn rate per settlement, on any `set_options`, `account_merge` or `payment` from our accounts, and on unexplained sequence jumps on channels.

**Recommendation.** Run OpenBao 2.7.x Transit as the facilitator signer on **both testnet and mainnet**, so mainnet never sees its signer for the first time. For mainnet, use three CX/CAX nodes in a spread placement group across FSN, NBG and HEL on one private network, separate from the facilitator hosts, with TLS certificate auth, a policy limited to `transit/sign/<key>`, no UI or OIDC, the audit log shipped off-host, monthly patching, and encrypted Raft snapshots on a timer. Generate the mainnet key inside Transit as non-exportable, and move the testnet key in through BYOK so it keeps its G-address. Unseal with Shamir (3-of-5 among the team) on mainnet, and accept the static seal on testnet. Treat the following as **mainnet blockers, independent of custody**: thresholds that keep the hot key below high on every channel and on the fee account, a cold recovery signer at high (paid for in reserve or sponsored), a small fee-account float with top-ups from a treasury key, alerts on audit-log sign rate against settlement count, and the `checkChannel` change. Make the signer client fail closed, with a timeout and a clear 503 when OpenBao is unreachable.

**What changes from ADR 0011.** SSM SecureString on testnet disappears, and testnet and mainnet no longer use different mechanisms. AWS KMS stops being the mainnet default and becomes a documented upgrade path behind the same `TransactionSigner`. Task 0036's two open questions, signature encoding and seed import, are now answered by documentation, so 0036 becomes a spike (sign and land a testnet fee bump with KMS through Roles Anywhere plus a TPM-held certificate, confirm an imported seed keeps its G-address, measure p50/p99 from FSN/NBG to eu-central-1) to run only if a trigger appears: a customer or regulator demanding HSM custody or FIPS, or an incident showing that OpenBao-node compromise is a live risk. Recovery stops being a property of the custody vendor and becomes an on-chain property: the cold signer applies whichever option holds the hot key. A superseding ADR should record all of this. Before relying on any of it, three measurements are missing: the facilitator-to-OpenBao latency over Hetzner's private network, whether OpenBao's Transit external key material supports Ed25519 (the route to hardware custody without changing the API), and whether 100 `SetOptions` operations fit in one transaction for batch rotation.

## Conclusion

The move to Hetzner changes the question as much as the answer. On AWS, KMS was the obvious and nearly free choice. Off AWS, its one unique advantage, a key that nobody can extract, has to be weighed against putting another cloud on every settlement. With the Stellar controls in place, that advantage shrinks: a key exfiltrated from an OpenBao node can sign no more than a hijacked KMS session can, because neither can touch signers, and one cold-signed batch transaction removes it. What separates the two options then is how long an attacker keeps access after detection and how much money is exposed, and both are set by rotation runbooks and float size, not by the custody vendor.

The sharper lesson is that our largest exposure today is neither the custody product nor the host. It is three thresholds left at zero. Fixing them, and adding the cold recovery signer, is cheap and works under any custody option. It also makes a later move from OpenBao to KMS or to an HSM a routine signer rotation rather than a migration. We should hold off on hardware custody until a customer, an auditor or an incident asks for it, and when one does, OpenBao's external key material and the dormant 0036 spike are both ready.
